## At a glance

**Context:** mutual authentication and secret-key establishment using pre-shared secrets, including resource-constrained environments.

**My role:** coauthor alongside Bruno Rohee, Michael Le Clerc and Clément Devun.

**Public deliverable:** the EAP-PSK-256 individual Internet-Draft, available on IETF Datatracker.

> This is a work-in-progress proposal. It is not an RFC, an adopted IETF standard or an IETF endorsement of the protocol’s security.

## The engineering question

How can a lightweight authentication method evolve its cryptographic primitives and key derivation while retaining the practical constraints of its target devices?

The draft explores an evolution of EAP-PSK, with attention to authentication, session-key establishment and the protected channel. Its quantum-resistance objective is part of the proposal, not a claim of independent certification.

## What the draft proposes

- **AES-256-based mechanisms** in place of the original AES-128-based construction.
- **Key derivation based on NIST SP 800-108**, using a specified KDF rather than treating key generation as an implementation detail.
- **Peer and server randomness** in session-key derivation.
- Explicit discussion of message flows, key hierarchy and security assumptions.

My contribution is part of this jointly authored document. The public record establishes coauthorship; the protocol should not be read as an individual invention or as work solely attributable to me.

## Why it matters in practice

A cryptographic transition is also a systems problem. Key provisioning, device resources, deployment compatibility and lifecycle management all affect whether a design can be operated securely.

For a security review, I would examine the quality and protection of pre-shared keys, random-number generation, implementation behavior and migration constraints alongside the choice of primitive.

## Boundaries and next questions

The draft explicitly does **not** provide perfect forward secrecy. A longer AES key does not remove risks from weak secrets, compromised endpoints or incorrect implementations.

This case study reports a public specification contribution. It does not claim production deployment, independent validation or measured performance. Those require their own evidence.
